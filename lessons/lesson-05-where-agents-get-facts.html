Lesson 5 · Part 1: Foundations

Where agents get facts

Talk + discussion

Goal: know the difference between searching the web and querying a database, and what an API is.

Why it matters

WebSearch is Google, not Westlaw. It finds public web pages; it doesn’t search scholarly or legal databases.

Searching the web vs querying a database

An agent can get outside facts in two very different ways. It can run a web search, the way you would in a browser, or it can ask a specific database for records.

Search engine (WebSearch)Database API
ReturnsPublic web pagesStructured records: title, authors, date, ID
CoverageBroadOnly that database
ReliabilityPages may be wrong, old, or AI-generatedRecords exist in the database
AnalogyGoogleWestlaw

One caution on that last “Reliability” cell. A database record proves a source exists. It doesn’t prove the source says what the agent claims it says. Both checks matter, the same way a cite-check confirms the case is real and that it stands for the proposition.

How a program asks a database: APIs

You use Westlaw through a website built for people. A program needs a door built for programs. That door is an API: the program sends a request in an exact format, and the database sends back records in a structured format (usually JSON) that the program can read.

Terms

API (application programming interface)
A set of web addresses a program can call to get data or take an action.
Endpoint
One of those addresses, like CourtListener’s citation lookup address, https://www.courtlistener.com/api/rest/v4/citation-lookup/.
API key (or token)
A password for a program. It identifies you and is sent with each request; for CourtListener, as the header Authorization: Token <your key>.
Rate limit
How many requests you may make in a period. Go over it and requests fail until you wait.

The database your agent will use

In lesson 10 your agent gets a small program, lookup.py, that queries one database: CourtListener. It replaces the web search your checker uses to confirm that cited cases exist. CourtListener isn’t the only free research API; the other two rows are examples from outside law.

Free doesn’t mean unlimited. CourtListener’s citation lookup handles up to 60 citations a minute, and its general limits for a signed-in user are much lower than you might expect: about 5 requests a minute, 50 an hour and 125 a day. arXiv asks programs to wait about three seconds between repeated calls. Keep lookups small.

Invented sources are a real risk

In Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), two lawyers submitted a court filing that cited judicial opinions that ChatGPT had made up, including Varghese v. China Southern Airlines Co., 925 F.3d 1339 (11th Cir. 2019). One of them told the court he had “falsely assumed” ChatGPT was “like a super search engine.” When he asked ChatGPT whether Varghese was a real case, it said yes.

Judge Castel was clear that the tool wasn’t the problem: “there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance. But existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings.” The lawyers and their firm were sanctioned for submitting the fake opinions and then standing by them after the court questioned whether they existed. The court imposed a $5,000 penalty and ordered them to send letters to their client and to each real judge falsely named as an author of a fake opinion.

Notice the lesson for agents: asking the model to check its own work didn’t help. The check has to go to a source outside the model. In lesson 9 you’ll plant a made-up case in a brief, Henderson v. Pickering, 5 U.S. 901, and see whether your checker catches it with a web search. In lesson 10 you’ll run the same test against CourtListener, and it should come back not found.

Even legal research tools hallucinate

It’s tempting to think the fix is simply to use a legal research product instead of a general chatbot. A Stanford RegLab study tested exactly that. The researchers wrote a preregistered set of 202 legal questions (general research questions, jurisdiction- or time-specific questions, questions with a false premise, and simple factual recall) and put them to the AI research tools from LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters, plus GPT-4 as a comparison.

Hallucination isn’t the only thing that matters. A tool that refuses to answer never hallucinates, but it also doesn’t help. So the study also measured how often each tool gave an answer that was both correct and properly supported:

ToolHallucinatedCorrect and grounded
Lexis+ AI17%65%
Ask Practical Law AI17%19%
Westlaw AI-Assisted Research33%41%
GPT-443%Not given as a number (figure only)

Source: Magesh et al., “Hallucination-Free?” (J. Empirical Legal Stud. 2025). Ask Practical Law AI’s low hallucination rate comes partly from giving incomplete answers or declining to answer on more than 60% of queries.

An earlier study by some of the same authors, “Large Legal Fictions” (Dahl, Magesh, Suzgun & Ho, J. Legal Analysis 2024), asked general-purpose chatbots specific, verifiable questions about randomly selected federal court cases. They hallucinated between 58% of the time (ChatGPT 4) and 88% of the time (Llama 2).

The takeaway isn’t “never use AI for research.” It’s that no tool’s output is a citation until you’ve checked it against the source, which is the job your brief-checker agent, and later lookup.py, will do.
