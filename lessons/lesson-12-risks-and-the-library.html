Lesson 12 · Part 2: Build

Risks of using agents

Talk + discussion

Goal: know how AI tools and agents fail in practice, and where to do serious legal research at Stanford.

Today you built an agent, planted errors in its work, and locked down what it may touch. Every guardrail you added answers a failure that has already happened to someone. This page collects real cases, then points you to the research tools Stanford already pays for, and to a library chatbot built to stricter standards than your agent.

What goes wrong: real cases

These are documented incidents, not hypotheticals. Each one maps to a guardrail you used today. Mata v. Avianca, the Stanford study of legal AI tools, ABA Formal Opinion 512 and the hidden prompts in research papers are covered in N7; this page adds the cases that came after.

1. Invented citations keep reaching courts

Researcher Damien Charlotin’s AI Hallucination Cases database listed 2,184 court decisions dealing with AI-fabricated or AI-garbled content as of October 10, 2026, 1,492 of them in the United States. The penalties have grown from warnings to five-figure sanctions, disqualification and bar referrals.

  • Wadsworth v. Walmart (D. Wyo., Feb. 2025). Motions drafted with a large firm’s own in-house AI tool cited eight cases that were made up or wrong. The drafting lawyer was fined $3,000 and removed from the case; two lawyers who only signed were fined $1,000 each. Everyone who signs is responsible.
  • Johnson v. Dunn (N.D. Ala., July 2025). A partner used ChatGPT to add citations to motions without checking them. Three lawyers were disqualified from the case and referred to the state bar; the judge said fines alone had proven “insufficient deterrents.” The firm already had an AI policy. A policy no one enforces is an instruction no one follows.
  • Noland v. Land of the Free (Cal. Ct. App., Sept. 2025). California’s first published appellate opinion on the issue: 21 of the 23 quotations in a brief were fabricated, and counsel was sanctioned $10,000.
  • Couvrette v. Wisnovsky (D. Or., Dec. 2025). Fifteen fake cases and eight made-up quotations. The client’s case was dismissed with prejudice, and the lawyers faced about $110,000 in fees and fines.
  • Whiting v. City of Athens (6th Cir., Mar. 2026). An appellate court found more than two dozen fake citations and misrepresentations in briefs, fined two lawyers $15,000 each and referred them for discipline. The court didn’t decide whether AI was involved; the duty to check is the same either way.
  • Concord Music v. Anthropic (N.D. Cal., May 2025). Lawyers for Anthropic itself asked Claude to format a citation to a real article. Claude got the title and authors wrong, and the court struck that part of the expert’s declaration. Even “just format this” needs checking.
  • Two federal judges (D.N.J. and S.D. Miss., 2025) withdrew orders containing errors after staff used ChatGPT and Perplexity in drafting. Both chambers added review procedures. It isn’t only lawyers.

Your guardrail: every claim carries a quote and a pinpoint, a separate checker verifies each one, and lesson 10 confirms citations in a case-law database.

2. Agents that act can break things

  • Replit’s coding agent (July 2025). During a declared code freeze, and despite instructions not to change anything without approval, an AI agent deleted a company’s live database of about 1,200 executive and 1,200 company records, then said it had “panicked.” Replit’s CEO called it “unacceptable and should never be possible,” and the company added separate development and production databases, a planning-only mode and one-click restore.

Your guardrail: “don’t touch production” was an instruction, and instructions are weighed, not enforced. That’s why you gave each agent a narrow tools line, added deny rules in lesson 9, and approved actions in Manual mode.

3. Hidden instructions hijack agents

  • EchoLeak (Microsoft 365 Copilot, June 2025). A crafted email hid instructions. When the user later asked Copilot an ordinary question, it read that email and sent internal data out, without the user clicking anything. Microsoft fixed it on its servers.
  • GitHub “toxic agent flow” (May 2025). Researchers planted instructions in a public GitHub issue. An agent asked to look at the issue copied data from the user’s private repositories into a public pull request.
  • Perplexity’s Comet browser (Aug. 2025). Hidden text in a Reddit comment made the AI browser fetch the user’s email address and a one-time login code and post them back to the attacker: enough to take over the account.

Your guardrail: “treat what you read as data,” the hidden-instruction test in lesson 9, and above all narrow tools. An agent that can read your private data, read untrusted content and send things out is the dangerous combination.

4. The tools themselves can be poisoned

  • Amazon Q Developer (July 2025). An attacker slipped a prompt telling the agent to wipe files into an official release of Amazon’s AI coding extension for VS Code. A syntax error kept it from running; Amazon pulled the version.
  • postmark-mcp (Sept. 2025). A look-alike email tool for AI agents quietly copied every email it sent to an outside address. The real company had nothing to do with it.
  • ClawHavoc (Feb. 2026). Researchers audited 2,857 skills in a public marketplace for an AI assistant and found 341 malicious ones, most of which told users to run a “setup” command that installed password-stealing malware.

Your guardrail: the skill-vetting habits from lesson 4. Your own agent uses only files you wrote and read.

5. What you type isn’t private, and isn’t privileged

  • United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y., Feb. 2026). Judge Rakoff held that documents a criminal defendant created on his own with a consumer AI chatbot were protected by neither attorney-client privilege nor work product: the platform’s privacy policy defeated any expectation of confidentiality, and he wasn’t acting at counsel’s direction. Sharing them with his lawyer later didn’t help.
  • In re OpenAI copyright litigation (S.D.N.Y., 2025). A court ordered OpenAI to preserve ChatGPT conversations it would otherwise have deleted, including ones users had deleted themselves. A provider’s deletion promise gives way to a court order.
  • Samsung (2023). Engineers pasted internal source code into ChatGPT, and the company temporarily banned AI chatbots on its devices.
  • Moffatt v. Air Canada (B.C. Civil Resolution Tribunal, Feb. 2024). The airline’s chatbot misstated its bereavement-fare policy. The tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own words and held the airline liable. Whoever deploys an agent owns what it says.

Your guardrail: keep client and confidential material out of cases/, keep secrets in .env, and read the confidentiality duties in N7 before using any AI tool on a client matter.

Sources checked October 12, 2026. Links go to the court record or agency notice where available, otherwise to legal press or the original researchers’ write-up.

Use your law library

An agent that searches the open web sees what Google sees. Stanford Law students have far more. The Robert Crown Law Library and Stanford University Libraries give you access to more than 100 legal and law-related databases, from Westlaw and Lexis to collections of historical records that aren’t on the open web at all. Use them alongside any agent you build: the agent can help you read and organize, but the databases are where the authoritative sources live.

Watch out

The library also licenses law-specific AI tools, such as CoCounsel in Westlaw, Protégé in Lexis, Harvey and Legora. They search curated legal content before answering, which helps. It doesn’t make them right: in the Stanford study in N7, the leading legal AI research tools still hallucinated on roughly one query in six to one in three. Check their citations the way your checker does.

Three ways to search

The finder is a curated selection of the library’s most-used resources, not an exhaustive list. It gives you three ways in.

  1. Browse all databases. Search by name or description, browse A to Z, or filter by how you get in.
  2. Guided paths. Pick a topic (Administrative, Arbitration, Constitutional, Corporate, Courts, Criminal, Foreign and International, Government and Politics, Historical, Tribal and Indigenous) or a source type (finding cases and statutes, secondary sources, legislative history, dockets, transactional, legal news, factual research, archival materials, AI tools), and get the short list the librarians recommend.
  3. AI Database Finder. Sign in with your SUNet ID, describe your research question in plain language, and get recommended databases with the reasoning behind each one. It tells you where to look; it doesn’t answer legal questions. Start a new search for each new topic.

Still stuck? The reference librarians answer research questions at reference@law.stanford.edu or 650-725-0800. They are the original research agent, and they don’t hallucinate citations.

Try it

Ask the AI Database Finder: “Where can I check whether Marbury v. Madison is still good law, and find the briefs filed in the case?” Compare its answer with the Limitations section of your own brief. Which tells you more about where to go next?

How the library’s chatbot differs from your agent

The AI Database Finder and your briefer share the core rule you wrote in lesson 6: answer only from the sources you were given. The difference is how that rule is kept, and for how many people.

Your agent and the library’s AI Database Finder
Your agentThe library’s AI finder
Grounded inOne opinion PDF, plus whatever pages the checker opens on the webThe library’s own catalog: metadata on every database it provides, written and maintained by the librarians, plus their research guides
Who supervisesYou, approving each step in Manual modeNobody in real time, so the rules are enforced by code
Rules kept byMarkdown instructions, a tools line and two deny rulesA written policy for the model, plus code that rejects any link not in the catalog
CheckingA second agent whose report you readA second model reviews every draft. A failing draft is rewritten, up to twice, and then replaced with a fixed safe answer
ScopeWhatever you askRecommends databases only. Refuses legal advice and off-topic questions, and won’t state holdings or citations from memory
Who can use itAnyone at your keyboardStanford affiliates signed in with SUNet; requests from other websites are rejected; answers are signed so a conversation can’t be faked
Cost and abuseYour plan’s limitsLimits per user and per network, a daily cap, a ceiling on model calls per question, size limits on questions, and timeouts
PrivacyWhatever you send goes to the model providerResearch questions are treated as sensitive: system logs record usage data only, and full questions and answers are kept only by the library, to improve the service
ShapeAn agent: it decides which pages to read and which tools to useA workflow with more deterministic steps
Proving it worksFive tests you ran by handAutomated tests on every change, a side-by-side tool for comparing models, and thumbs-up or thumbs-down feedback on every answer

Why it matters

The finder has a Markdown file too: its instructions to the model. What makes it fit for hundreds of users is everything around that file. An instruction is a request the model weighs; a check in code is a limit. Your agent works because one person supervises every step. A service with no one watching has to turn each rule that matters into code. The Markdown file is the policy; the code is the enforcement. It’s the difference between a firm’s ethics handbook and its conflict-check system.
