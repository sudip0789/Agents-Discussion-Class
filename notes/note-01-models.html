Concept note N1

Models

In one line: a model predicts the next piece of text; everything else in an AI product is built around it.

ChatGPT, Claude and Claude Code are apps. Inside each one is a model: a program that reads text and predicts what comes next, one small piece at a time. The app wraps the model in instructions, tools, memory and a chat window. Knowing which abilities belong to the model and which belong to the app explains most of what AI tools get right and wrong.

The vocabulary at a glance

Each term gets a fuller explanation further down the page.

Terms

Model (LLM)
A large language model: the trained program that predicts the next piece of text. ChatGPT and Claude are apps built around models.
Token
The unit a model reads and writes. As a rough rule of thumb, a token is about three-quarters of an English word, but it varies by model and language. Context limits and prices are counted in tokens.
Training and parameters
Training adjusts billions of numbers, the parameters, until the model predicts text well. After training they’re fixed.
Fine-tuning
Training a model further on new examples, which changes its parameters. Prompts, skills and instruction files guide a model without changing it.
Context window
Everything the model can see at once: instructions, the conversation so far, and every file an agent reads.
System prompt
Instructions the app gives the model before your message, usually hidden from you. Agent files and CLAUDE.md work the same way.
Knowledge cutoff
The date where the training data stops. Anything newer reaches the model only through tools like search.
Sampling and temperature
The model picks among likely next tokens with some randomness, so the same prompt can give different answers. Temperature sets how much randomness.
Hallucination
Confident output that isn’t true, such as an invented case or a paper that doesn’t exist.
Reasoning (“thinking”)
The model writes scratch work before its answer. Better on hard problems, but slower and costlier. In Claude Code, effort settings control how much it thinks.
Model tiers
Companies offer larger and smaller models. Claude comes as Opus, Sonnet and Haiku. Larger is more capable, slower and costlier.
Open-weight vs closed
Open-weight models can be downloaded and run on your own computer. That isn’t the same as open source: the training data and code can stay private.
Multimodal
The model can read images and PDFs, not only text.

How a model is made

Training and parameters

A model starts as billions of random numbers, its parameters. During training, it reads an enormous amount of text and repeatedly guesses the next token. Each wrong guess nudges the parameters a little, so the next guess is slightly better. After trillions of guesses, the parameters encode patterns of grammar, facts, reasoning and style.

When training ends, the parameters are frozen. Nothing you type changes them. The model you talk to on Monday is the same model on Friday, even if you corrected it a dozen times in between.

Fine-tuning vs instructions

There are two ways to change how a model behaves, and they’re easy to confuse:

Fine-tuningInstructions
Changes the parameters?YesNo
ExamplesA company training a model on legal documentsPrompts, skills, agent files, CLAUDE.md
Who does itThe company, or a developer with training data and computing powerAnyone who can type
How long it lastsPermanently, in that version of the modelOnly while the text is in the context window

Everything you build in this class is the second kind. Your agent files, skills and CLAUDE.md are instructions: they guide the model each time it runs, and they work only because the app puts them into the context window.

Tokens

Models don’t read letters or words. They read tokens: chunks of text from a fixed vocabulary, usually a whole common word, part of a longer word, a number or a punctuation mark. As a rough rule of thumb from OpenAI, a token is about ¾ of an English word, so 100 tokens is about 75 words. That ratio varies: Anthropic notes that its newer Claude models split the same text into roughly 30% more tokens than earlier ones, and other languages often need more tokens per word.

Why it matters

Legal text is token-hungry. Citations, section symbols, pin cites and Latin phrases split into many small tokens, so a 40-page brief fills more of the context window, and costs more, than 40 pages of plain prose. Splitting text into pieces is also part of why models are bad at tasks like counting letters or checking exact page numbers: they never see the characters one by one.

The context window

The context window is the model’s whole working desk: everything it can see while writing its next token. It has a fixed size, measured in tokens. Anything not on the desk doesn’t exist for the model, and anything that is on the desk competes for its attention.

In an agent like Claude Code, the desk fills up fast. Before you type a word, it holds the app’s system prompt, your CLAUDE.md, and the descriptions of your tools and skills. Every message, every file the agent opens and every web page it fetches is added on top.

Three practical consequences:

  • Long sessions get worse, not better. As the window fills, older details are summarized (Claude Code calls this compacting) or simply get less attention. Instructions you gave an hour ago can fade.
  • /clear is your friend. It empties the conversation and starts fresh. Anything worth keeping should live in a file, where the agent can read it again.
  • Every always-loaded thing has a cost. A long CLAUDE.md or a dozen installed skills take space in every session, before you’ve asked anything. In Claude Code, /context shows how the window is being used.

The system prompt

The system prompt is the first thing in the window: instructions from the app, placed before your message and usually hidden from you. It tells the model who it is, what tools it has, today’s date, and how to behave. When you write CLAUDE.md, an agent file or a skill, you are writing more of the same thing: text the app slips into the context window ahead of your request.

The knowledge cutoff

A model’s knowledge cutoff is the date its training data ends. It has never seen anything newer: a case decided last month, a rule amended last week, a paper posted yesterday. Worse, it doesn’t always know that it doesn’t know. Ask about a recent event and it may answer from older patterns. Anything current has to come in through a tool, such as web search or a database query.

Sampling and temperature

At each step the model doesn’t produce one answer. It produces a probability for every token in its vocabulary, then the app samples one, like drawing from a weighted hat. Temperature sets how the weights are used. Low temperature sharpens the odds toward the likeliest token; high temperature flattens them so less likely tokens get picked more often.

Two things follow. First, the same prompt can give different answers, which is fine for brainstorming and a problem for anything you’ll cite. Second, a low temperature makes a model consistent, not correct: if the most likely continuation is wrong, it will be wrong every time. Developers can set temperature through the API; in chat apps and Claude Code you usually can’t.

Hallucination

A hallucination is confident output that isn’t true. It isn’t a glitch; it’s the same mechanism working as designed. The model produces the text that is most likely to come next. A case citation that looks right, with a plausible party name, reporter and page, is very likely text, whether or not the case exists.

The most famous legal example is Mata v. Avianca, Inc., No. 22-cv-1461 (PKC) (S.D.N.Y. June 22, 2023), where lawyers filed a brief citing cases ChatGPT had invented, including “Varghese v. China Southern Airlines Co., 925 F.3d 1339 (11th Cir. 2019).” The court imposed a $5,000 penalty. The lesson for agents: a model can’t vouch for its own sources. Something outside the model, such as a database lookup, an opened link or you, has to check.

Watch out

Hallucinations aren’t limited to fake cases. Real cases with the wrong holding, real papers with the wrong authors, and accurate quotes with invented page numbers are all common, and harder to spot.

Tip

When an AI tool surprises you, good or bad, ask: was that the model, or something the app gave it? “It looked it up” means a tool ran. “It knew” means it predicted. Only the first one can be checked.

How next-token prediction actually works

Modern language models are built on an architecture called the transformer, introduced by Google researchers in 2017. In very rough outline:

  1. Tokens become numbers. Each token is turned into a long list of numbers (a vector) that captures something about its meaning. Words used in similar ways end up with similar vectors.
  2. Attention mixes in context. Layer after layer, each token’s vector is updated by “attending” to the other tokens in the window. That is how “granted” in “the court granted” comes to mean something different from “granted” in “take it for granted.”
  3. The last vector becomes probabilities. After the final layer, the model converts the vector at the end of the text into a probability for every token in its vocabulary. That is the distribution in the bar chart above.
  4. Sample, append, repeat. One token is chosen, added to the text, and the whole process runs again for the next one.

The parameters are the numbers inside steps 1 to 3. Training tunes them; the architecture stays the same.

After this pretraining on raw text, companies train the model further on examples of helpful conversations and on human (or AI) ratings of its answers, often called RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback). That second stage is what turns a text predictor into an assistant that follows instructions, and it is a form of fine-tuning.

The 3Blue1Brown videos and Jay Alammar’s Illustrated Transformer in the reading list below show all of this visually.

How often do legal AI tools hallucinate?

Two Stanford studies measured it directly. The first tested general-purpose chatbots on verifiable questions about federal case law and found hallucination rates between 58% (GPT-4) and 88% (Llama 2) (Dahl, Magesh, Suzgun & Ho, “Large Legal Fictions,” J. Legal Analysis, 2024).

The second tested purpose-built legal research tools, which use retrieval from real legal databases, and found they still hallucinate, though less often:

Share of answers containing a hallucination
ToolHallucinated
Lexis+ AI17%
Ask Practical Law AI17%
Westlaw AI-Assisted Research33%
GPT-4 (no retrieval)43%

Source: Magesh, Surani, Dahl, Suzgun, Manning & Ho, “Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools,” J. Empirical Legal Stud. 22(2): 216–242 (2025), Table B1.

Accuracy and hallucination are different measures. In the same study, Ask Practical Law AI hallucinated rarely but gave incomplete answers or refused on more than 60% of queries. A tool that says less isn’t necessarily more useful.

The takeaway for your agent: retrieval (looking things up) reduces hallucination but doesn’t eliminate it. That is why your project adds a separate checker and a database lookup instead of trusting any single step.
